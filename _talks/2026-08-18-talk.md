## Abstract
Large spectropolarimetric surveys have robustly established an incidence rate of less than 10% for strong surface magnetic fields among Galactic main-sequence O and B-type massive stars. These magnetic fields are understood to play a crucial role in setting the physical properties and evolutionary paths of these stars, largely through the confinement of the stellar wind into co-rotating magnetospheres and the removal of angular momentum through magnetic braking. Due to these effects, magnetic massive stars are proposed as the main-sequence progenitors of diverse stellar end-products and phenomena, including heavy stellar mass black holes, pair-instability supernovae, magnetars, and fast radio bursts.  

Recent observational and theoretical studies highlight the need to integrate studies of stellar magnetism more completely into the broader landscape of massive star astrophysics. Until recently, massive star magnetic studies have been primarily limited to the characterization of photospheric and circumstellar magnetic fields. Cutting-edge magnetoasteroseismic techniques have begun to probe internal magnetic fields throughout the interiors of massive stars down to the convective core, where magnetic fields are expected to be ubiquitous.  Furthermore, recent studies point to an intricate relationship between magnetic field generation and binary mass-transfer and merger processes. In addition, deep spectropolarimetric magnetic detections of blue and yellow-supergiants reveal uncertainty surrounding magnetic field generation and decay mechanisms in evolved intermediate- and high-mass stars.

In this presentation, we highlight recent key observational studies that link binary interactions, magnetic field generation and the formation of highly magnetic compact remnants. We summarize advancements in the study of evolved massive stars and demonstrate how magnetic field measurements can provide direct, unambiguous measurements of stellar rotation periods at later evolutionary stages. Finally, we promote discussion on how to link observational magnetic studies with theoretical modelling, and how to leverage the next generation of spectroscopic and photometric surveys to answer these key questions.
