I am an observational stellar astrophysicist and [NSERC PGS-D](https://nserc-crsng.canada.ca/en/funding-opportunity/archived-nserc-postgraduate-scholarships-doctoral-program) supported PhD candidate in the Department of Physics, Engineering Physics & Astronomy at [Queen's University](https://www.queensu.ca/physics/people-search/james-barron), Canada. My PhD supervisor is [Dr. Gregg Wade](https://www.rmc-cmr.ca/en/physics-space-science/gregg-wade). 

## Research
My research seeks to answer fundamental questions about the origin, impact, and evolution of stellar magnetic fields. In particular, I am interested in the complex relationship between magnetic fields and stellar processes, including pulsation, rotation, mass loss and binary interactions. Less than $10\%$ of hot, massive stars on host strong, globally ordered magnetic fields. However, little is known about the origin of these fields and how they may impact and evolve in later stellar evolutionary stages. My work spans the upper Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, from hot main-sequence OB-type stars through later evolutionary stages including classical Cepheids. 

In massive stars, I study the fossil fields of hot, magnetic O and B-type stars and their connection to binary interactions and stellar mergers — including as a contributor to the discovery, published in [*Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adg7700), that the magnetic O star HD 148937 is the product of a stellar merger. My spectropolarimetric surveys of evolved stars extend this work along the HR diagram, and include the first direct measurement of the rotation period of a classical Cepheid, [Polaris](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae77f9).



 The principal tool of my research is [spectropolarimetry](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.00179), which enables the detection and characterization of stellar magnetic fields to [sub-Gauss precision](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stac565). I interpret these magnetic fields in the context of individual stellar systems and broader stellar populations through complementary analysis of time-series photometry and high-resolution spectroscopy. My work utilizes diverse astronomical observations, from ground-based facilities including the [Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope](https://www.cfht.hawaii.edu/), the [ESO 3.6-m Telescope](https://www.eso.org/public/teles-instr/lasilla/36/), and the [Gemini Observatory](https://www.gemini.edu/), as well as space missions such as [TESS](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/tess/), [Kepler](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/kepler/), and [Gaia](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia). The results of observational magnetic studies are used to test and refine theoretical stellar models.

My research primarily (but not [exclusively](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae2fb4)) focuses on the study of massive and pulsating stars many times larger than the sun. I have led and contributed to studies of hot, massive O and B-type stars that are extreme such as their [mass](63 Oph link), [rapid rotation](HR 5907 link) or exhibit signs of [merger generated fields](HD 148937 link). In addition, a key component of my PhD thesis is a first, deep spectroplarimetric study of pulsating classical Cepheid stars. Classical Cepheids are a rare class of intermediate- and high-mass stars which are famous for their role in measuring the Hubble constant, the expansion rate of the Universe. My most recent paper in this series is on [Polaris](link). 


My research seeks to answer fundamental questions about the origin, impact, and evolution of stellar magnetic fields. Roughly 10% of massive OB stars host strong, stable magnetic fields, yet far less is known about how these fields originate, evolve, and survive into later evolutionary stages.
